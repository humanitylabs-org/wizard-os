# Laozi — The Use of What Is Not There
*Tao Te Ching, chapter 11, tr. James Legge (1891) · ~5 minutes*

## In their own words
> The thirty spokes unite in the one nave; but it is on the empty space (for the axle), that the use of the wheel depends. Clay is fashioned into vessels; but it is on their empty hollowness, that their use depends. The door and windows are cut out (from the walls) to form an apartment; but it is on the empty space (within), that its use depends. Therefore, what has a (positive) existence serves for profitable adaptation, and what has not that for (actual) usefulness. [1]

*Translation by James Legge, 1891, from Project Gutenberg eBook #216, public domain in the USA. The words in parentheses are Legge's own additions to make the terse original clear. They are not part of the Chinese text.* [1]

## The compression
**My compression: You make a thing out of material, but you use it through the gap the material leaves. Build the frame for the sake of the empty part.**

## How this mind moves
**It starts with objects anyone can touch.** The chapter doesn't open with a theory of being. It opens with a wheel, a pot and a room [1]. Each one is something people make, and each is described the same way: first the material that is joined or shaped or cut, then a "but" that moves the use somewhere else. The spokes "unite in the one nave" (the hub), "but" the wheel works because of "the empty space (for the axle)" [1]. Laozi never says the spokes don't matter. He says the wheel's use "depends" on the hole they surround. Because the examples are so plain, the claim is hard to brush off. You can check it against any cup you own.

**It changes what counts as making.** Look at the verbs: spokes "unite", clay "is fashioned", doors and windows "are cut out" [1]. The room is the clearest case, because there you make the useful thing by taking material away. That puts the maker's skill in a new light. A potter isn't really making clay. The potter is shaping a hollow and using clay to hold its edges. This is my reading, not a fact about the text. But the wording points that way: in all three examples, use is tied to "empty space" or "empty hollowness" [1].

**It ends with a split job, not a ranking.** The closing sentence is easy to misread as a verdict that emptiness is good and substance is bad. What it actually says is that what exists "serves for profitable adaptation," and what does not exist serves "for (actual) usefulness" [1]. Both have a role. The strongest objection to a simple reading comes from the text itself: an axle-hole with no spokes around it isn't a wheel, it's just air. Someone defending the material could say emptiness is only useful because something bounds it, so the material comes first. The chapter's answer, as I read it, isn't to deny that. It's to point out what we tend to overlook. We praise, price and admire the clay. We almost never notice the hollow, though the hollow is why we wanted the pot.

**Keep three claims apart.** The historical claim is narrow: this passage appears in the Tao Te Ching, and Legge translated it this way in 1891 [1]. The interpretive claim is mine: the chapter moves usefulness from substance to the space substance frames. The normative claim goes beyond the text: we should design and judge things with that in mind. The source supports the first claim directly and the second reasonably. The third is an argument you'll have to test for yourself.

## Why today
Much modern work is judged by what it adds: features shipped, pages written, items on the calendar. Chapter 11 offers a different test. A calendar packed with meetings looks productive, but the work happens in the unbooked hours those meetings leave around them. A screen covered in controls looks capable, but the user can only act in the clear space where their attention can land. A room crammed with furniture has lost the open floor that made it a room. In each case the visible material is real, and it is the part most easily counted and rewarded. Laozi's point is that when you count it, you may be measuring the clay and ignoring the pot.

In practice, this changes the question you ask when reviewing something you've built. Don't just ask what you can add. Ask what open space this is supposed to protect, and whether that space is still there.

## Carry this question
In the thing I'm building right now, what is the empty part meant to be used for, and am I filling it in?
