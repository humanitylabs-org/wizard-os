# Laozi — The Empty Hub
*Tao Te Ching, chapter 11, tr. James Legge (1891) · ~5 minutes*

## In their own words
> The thirty spokes unite in the one nave; but it is on the empty space (for the axle), that the use of the wheel depends. [1]

Translation by James Legge (1891), Project Gutenberg eBook #216, public domain in the USA. [1]

## The compression
**My compression: What you build gives a thing its shape. What you leave empty is what lets it work.**

## How this mind moves
**It starts with the object in your hand.** The chapter makes no claim about the cosmos, the sage or the state. It gives three made things: a wheel, a clay vessel and a room. [1] In Legge's English the "nave" is the hub, the centre piece the thirty spokes run into. Every example is something a reader has touched today or will touch by tonight. Your morning cup has a glazed wall, a weight and a colour. All of that is what you paid for and what you notice. What holds the coffee, though, is the hollow, the part of the cup that has no material in it at all. The text keeps the reader on familiar objects and then turns them over to show the side we don't look at.

**Then it repeats the same turn three times.** Each example follows one pattern: something gets made, and then comes a "but". The spokes come together, *but* the wheel's use depends on the empty space for the axle. The clay is shaped, *but* the use depends on the hollowness. The doors and windows are cut out of the walls, *but* the use depends on the empty space inside. [1] Repeating the pattern is how the argument works. One example could be a clever observation. Three examples, taken from transport, storage and shelter, start to look like a rule about making things in general. Notice also that the doors and windows are themselves gaps cut into the wall. [1] Even the openings that make a room usable are absences.

**Last, it divides the work instead of ranking it.** The last sentence is easy to misread as a verdict that emptiness is good and matter is bad. It actually splits the labour in two: what exists "serves for profitable adaptation", and what does not exist serves "for (actual) usefulness." [1] The two depend on each other. A hole with no wall around it isn't a cup, just air. Here is the strongest objection to the chapter: the potter's skill, the wheelwright's precision and the mason's labour all go into the solid parts, so isn't it perverse to give the credit to nothing? Laozi's text can survive that objection, because it never says the solid parts are useless. It says their value comes from the shape they give to the empty part. One note on reading: the words in parentheses, such as "(for the axle)" and "(positive)", look like clarifications Legge added to his English. So how much that last sentence leans toward a neat opposition depends partly on the translator's choices. That is an interpretive caution, not a claim about the Chinese original, which the supplied source doesn't include.

## Why today
Most of what we measure is the solid part. We count features shipped, meetings held and square metres furnished. The chapter points at what those measures leave out. A calendar packed with commitments has the same problem as a cup filled with clay: everything is substance and nothing can be held. A kitchen counter covered in appliances has lost the empty surface that made it a place to cook. Applied to ordinary decisions, the chapter's claim says that taking something away, such as clearing a shelf, leaving a free hour or keeping a doorway open, can make a thing more useful, even though nothing visible was added. The reverse is also true: if you fill the hollow, the thing stops working, and it can look as impressive as before while it does.

## Carry this question
In something you use every day, where is the hollow that makes it work, and are you about to fill it?
